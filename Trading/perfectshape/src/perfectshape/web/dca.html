1. Borrowing sized against the account: crash, VIX, news and trend rules

What if the saver may borrow more than six payments: 10% of the account after a 10% crash, half of it when the VIX spikes, and may sell to repay? Each rule sets the account's exposure (position over equity: 1 = plain DCA, 1.5 = borrowed half the equity, 0.5 = half in cash). Two questions per rule: how much does it beat plain DCA, and does it fall further than plain DCA did over the same years?

Drawdown

The same rules on S&P 1928-1961 (held out)

  • Buying the crash on margin never reduced the drawdown. Borrowing 10% after a 10% fall beat DCA in about two accounts in three, by about 1%, and fell deeper in every one of them. Bigger ladders earn more on average and lose 20% or more against DCA when the crash keeps going (1973, 2000, 2008).
  • VIX, volatility spikes and policy news pick good moments on average but not reliably: about half to two thirds of accounts, with deeper falls.
  • Only one family beats DCA and falls less: borrowed while the index is above its 200-day average, partly in cash below it. The borrowing pays for the cash; the cash is what cuts the fall.
  • It trades a lot (the index crosses its average often) and it sells, so taxes and costs matter: at 0.2% a trade its lead shrinks by more than half. It did not work on the FTSE 100 and only half the time on the DAX.

2. The six-payment strategy: skip or borrow up to six payments, never sell

The rule

    Against plain DCA, for every start month

    One saver is one sample of luck. Each row opens an account on every payment day in the data, runs it for that many years, and compares it with a plain-DCA account opened the same day. Edge = final wealth over plain DCA's, minus 1.

    Edge of a 10-year account, by the month it was opened
    One saver

    What was tried

    10-year accounts, every start month since 1962, the same account rules for all. Holding payments back loses; borrowing all the time wins on average and fails in whole decades; borrowing only above the average is what holds up.

    Why it works

    Borrowing a payment pays when stocks beat the loan's rate. Over 98 years they did so, by a wide and steady margin, in months that began with the index above its 200-day average, and not reliably in months that began below it, where the swings were nearly twice as large.

    Read this before trusting it

    • The prize is small by construction. Six payments is a sliver of a grown account. A saver who knew every future price would gain about 14% over ten years; this rule gains 2 to 3%.
    • It is leverage, timed. While borrowed, the account holds up to six payments more stock than the DCA saver. A crash that starts above the average (1929, 1987) hits before the rule steps back; a young account feels it most, which is why 5-year results are weaker than 10-year ones.
    • The margin rate matters. The study borrows at the 10-year Treasury yield, as specified. Most brokers charge more; the table above shows 1, 2 and 3 points more.
    • Not a law of nature. It works on the S&P 500 in every era tested, on the Nasdaq-100 and the DAX; it adds little or nothing on the Russell 2000, FTSE 100 and Nikkei (price indices without dividends, US rates).
    • Chosen with hindsight. The 200-day average is the textbook setting and 1928-1961 was held out, but the rate guard and the debt limit were picked after seeing 1962-2026. The untouched test starts now.
    • Daily closes, payments on the first trading day of the month, no taxes, interest accrued daily, dividends reinvested through the total-return index.
