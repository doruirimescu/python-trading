Following the Big Money

Is a U.S. sector rotation already underway? A plain-English, data-backed examination of prices, ETF creations and redemptions, hedge-fund positioning, dark-pool volume and corporate insider transactions.

The conclusion first

There are credible early signs that large investors are reducing crowded semiconductor, hardware and high-beta software positions and reallocating toward healthcare, consumer staples, energy and real estate. The evidence is strong enough to justify a small, staged rebalance for an investor who is materially overweight technology—but not strong enough to justify abandoning the sector or making an all-in defensive bet.

Base-case interpretation

The movement is best described as deconcentration and profit protection. Broad technology exposure is not being liquidated uniformly: the broad XLK fund still received money while semiconductor and software funds suffered billions of dollars of redemptions. That distinction matters.

What "sector rotation" actually means

A sector rotation occurs when investors reduce exposure to one part of the market and increase exposure elsewhere. It does not require the whole market to fall. The S&P 500 can remain stable—or even rise—while leadership moves from growth industries such as semiconductors into steadier businesses such as pharmaceuticals, insurers or food producers.

Three concepts are easy to confuse:

SignalWhat it measuresWhat it does not prove
Price performanceWhich sectors investors are currently rewarding.Whether new cash entered or existing owners simply refused to sell.
ETF net flowNet fund-share creation or redemption; a closer approximation of capital entering or leaving a strategy.Every transaction in the underlying stocks or the investor's motivation.
Trading volumeIntensity of trading and attention.Whether the volume was initiated by buyers or sellers.

This article therefore requires agreement across several independent datasets. No single green or red number is allowed to carry the conclusion.

Fund flows provide the strongest confirmation

Price changes can be caused by relatively little trading. ETF creations and redemptions are more informative because they measure whether fund shares were issued or removed. The technology exit is concentrated, not universal

Within technology, SOXX lost $2.78 billion, IGV software lost $1.23 billion, SMH lost $1.11 billion and global technology fund IXN lost $514 million. Yet broad XLK received $276 million. Healthcare's XLV received $361 million, XLP received $314 million and IYH received $258 million.

This is the central nuance of the entire analysis: the evidence supports a reduction in crowded, higher-beta technology exposures—not indiscriminate liquidation of all profitable technology companies.

Why the timing looks early

For the week through 22 July, LSEG Lipper still reported $1.17 billion entering U.S. technology-sector funds, alongside $1.39 billion for financials and $1.35 billion for healthcare. The abrupt five-day technology redemptions that followed therefore represent a genuine short-horizon reversal rather than a trend that has already persisted for many months.

Broad market liquidity has not disappeared. The Investment Company Institute reported $31.78 billion of total ETF net issuance for the week ending 22 July even as long-term mutual funds lost $34.95 billion. Capital is still available; it is becoming more selective.

Hedge funds show the move began before the headlines

Goldman Sachs prime-brokerage data, reported by Reuters on 6 July, showed U.S. hedge funds selling technology hardware for a fourth consecutive week. Information technology—including semiconductors and hardware—was the most net-sold U.S. sector, while industrials and consumer discretionary were also sold. The same investors bought commercial services, consumer staples, real estate and energy.

That history matters because it changes the diagnosis. The late-July price break did not create the institutional rotation; it made an existing reduction in exposure visible to everyone else.

Healthcare offers an even clearer incoming signal. By the week of 24 July, hedge-fund exposure to U.S. healthcare relative to equities was near a five-year high for a second consecutive week. Buying centered on equipment and supplies, life-science tools and pharmaceuticals. This is broader than a single insurer or drugmaker rally.

Important limitation: semiconductor exposure remained historically elevated even after several weeks of selling. Large investors are taking chips off the table, not necessarily rejecting the long-term AI investment theme.

Sources: Reuters on Goldman prime-brokerage technology flows; Goldman Sachs on semiconductor profit-taking; Reuters on near-five-year-high healthcare positioning.

Dark pools: useful context, not a directional oracle

Alternative trading systems, often called dark pools, allow institutions to trade without displaying the full order publicly before execution. FINRA publishes weekly aggregate shares and trade counts, but Tier 1 securities appear with roughly a two-week delay; Tier 2 data arrive after roughly four weeks. Most importantly, the public data do not identify buyer-initiated versus seller-initiated volume.

The latest Tier 1 dataset available for this study covers the week beginning 6 July and was published on 27 July. The table below compares each sector SPDR's ATS share volume with its preceding four-week average.

Insiders do not confirm the sector thesis

Corporate insiders are not portfolio managers rotating money between sectors. They sell for taxes, diversification, option exercises and personal liquidity; many transactions execute under plans arranged well in advance. Insider purchases are generally more informative than sales because an executive has many reasons to sell but usually only one economic reason to buy: expected value.

The overall July insider buy/sell ratio was approximately 0.25, down from 0.27. Recent large filings included substantial sales or proposed sales in SNOW, MU, CRWV, DDOG and other technology companies. But healthcare also contained major selling in MEDP, ONC, ADPT and UTHR, despite a notable approximately $36.1 million director purchase in SCTX. Staples were mixed as well, including an approximately $2 million ACI purchase alongside sizable Coca-Cola sale filings.

The correct conclusion is not "insiders are bearish on everything." It is that the filings fail to show the broad, repeated purchase clusters that would independently validate a sector rotation.

The SEC's Rule 10b5-1 framework also explains why a sale appearing during a market decline may reflect a previously adopted plan rather than a fresh bearish decision. The SEC strengthened cooling-off periods and disclosure requirements, but planned trades still must be interpreted differently from discretionary open-market purchases.

Sources: Finviz recent-week Form 4 transactions; GuruFocus market insider summary; SEC Rule 10b5-1 amendments.

An early, reversible rebalancing framework

For a hobby investor, the practical lesson is not to chase whichever sector was green yesterday. It is to reduce accidental concentration and let stronger evidence earn a larger allocation over time.

Measure the existing technology overweight.

Compare technology, semiconductor and software exposure across every fund you own. A broad index fund plus a Nasdaq fund plus a semiconductor ETF can create far more concentration than the account labels suggest.

Make only one-third of the intended change now.

For an investor with a meaningful overweight, an illustrative first move is 2–4 percentage points of the total portfolio—not a wholesale sector switch. Fund it by trimming the concentrated semiconductor or high-beta software sleeve, not necessarily profitable broad-market technology holdings.

Allocate the first tranche toward the best-confirmed destinations.

An illustrative 3-point shift could add roughly 1.25 points to healthcare, 0.75 to consumer staples, 0.50 to energy and 0.50 to real estate. Materials belongs on a watch list until price confirms the unusual volume and positive flow.

Require confirmation before a second tranche.

Add only if healthcare and staples retain relative strength and their category flows remain positive over another full week. Avoid increasing financials merely because their trailing-month price is strong: the newest five-day fund flow was negative by more than $1 billion.

Use explicit invalidation rules.

Stop increasing the tilt if technology recovers more than half of its category outflow within ten trading days, if XLV and XLP both surrender their weekly relative gains, or if the next two weekly flow reports show defensive redemptions.

Keep the core diversified.

A sector-rotation signal should affect the satellite portion of a long-term portfolio. Taxes, trading costs, time horizon and the ability to tolerate a technology rebound can matter more than a short-term signal score.

Why stage the trade? A 62–76% confidence band implies meaningful edge but substantial failure risk. A partial first tranche participates if the trend continues while preserving capital and flexibility if technology snaps back.

Final assessment

The evidence supports an early but already multi-week rotation. The outgoing side is clearest in semiconductors, technology hardware, high-beta software, consumer discretionary and industrials. The incoming side is clearest in healthcare and consumer staples, with secondary support for energy and real estate.

Financials are not a clean incoming sector despite positive recent performance because their newest ETF flows are strongly negative. Utilities are not participating in the defensive move. Materials are an intriguing but unconfirmed candidate: ETF inflows and unusual ATS volume have appeared before price leadership.

The best summary is:

Crowded AI and semiconductor exposure → healthcare and staples, with smaller allocations toward energy and real estate.

This is currently a portfolio deconcentration trade—not evidence that technology's long-run earnings advantage or the AI investment cycle has ended.

A hobby investor following the signal should rebalance gradually, begin with concentration control, and demand another week of corroborating flows before making a larger move. The data justify preparation and a small first tranche. They do not justify certainty.
